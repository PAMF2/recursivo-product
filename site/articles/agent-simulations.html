How agent simulations of people work.

Generative agents, personas, and digital twins are the machinery behind a multi-billion-dollar category. This is how they are built, and why the most common architecture predicts one real person correctly only about a third of the time.

A category worth billions of dollars rests on one promise: software that predicts how real people will behave. Underneath the branding, agentic twins, synthetic respondents, simulated populations, sit a small number of concrete architectures. This is the category RecursivoBench grades, so it is worth knowing exactly how these simulators are built, and exactly where they break.

What an agent simulation is

An agent simulation of a person is a program that takes a description of who someone is and a question they might be asked, and returns the answer that person would give. Strip away the vocabulary and the whole field reduces to one interface. A simulator is anything that reads a persona, a question, and a set of options, and returns exactly one of those options: the choice this person would make. A persona-prompt, an open generative-agent stack, a neuro-symbolic engine, or a closed vendor API all fit behind that one function. That is what makes them comparable on a single ruler.

The simplest and most common version is the persona-prompt. You paste a description of the person, up to around fifteen hundred characters, into a language model, instruct it to answer as that person would, and force it to reply with exactly one option from the list and nothing else. Most products sold as "synthetic respondents" are precisely this, stripped of branding. It is the lowest-fidelity approach in the field and also the one the field leans on hardest.

The five architectures

Across roughly twenty-five players we mapped in our category research, the field collapses into five approaches:

  • Language model plus persona-prompt. The most common and the lowest-fidelity: a description of a person handed to a language model that answers in their voice.
  • Neuro-symbolic. Explicit behavioral rules combined with a language model, so hand-written logic constrains what the model is allowed to say.
  • Digital twin, one to one. A model fit to one specific real respondent's answer history, aimed at reproducing that single person rather than a type.
  • Generative agents with memory and reflection. The "agentic twin" line, built on the generative-agents architecture from Park et al. (UIST 2023).
  • Agent-based emergent. Many interacting agents whose group behavior emerges from local rules, used to model whole populations rather than named individuals.

Generative agents, in detail

The architecture that gives the category its "agentic" language layers three moving parts on top of a language model:

  • Memory stream. A long, timestamped log of everything the agent has observed or done. Entries are retrieved by a mix of recency, importance, and relevance to the current situation.
  • Reflection. Periodically the agent summarizes its raw memories into higher-level inferences, statements like "I care about X", which feed back into the stream as new, more abstract entries.
  • Planning. The agent turns reflections and memories into plans and actions, which produce new observations, which extend the memory stream. The loop repeats.

The point of all this is coherence over time: an agent that remembers what it did yesterday and acts consistently today. It is a genuinely useful architecture for interactive simulation. Whether it predicts a specific real person's survey answer is a separate question, and it is the question the category never grades independently.

You do not have to guess how the leading agentic twin is built, because the generative-agent stack behind it has been open-sourced. It implements the same memory-stream, reflection, and planning machinery described above. We cloned that open stack, wired its model calls to our own backend, and ran it on the benchmark end to end. A sample agent answered a categorical question, "Somewhat support" on a carbon tax, through the full memory-and-reflection pipeline, which proves the integration works and lets us grade the open architecture on the same ruler as everything else.

Why they look right in aggregate

These systems are convincing in demos because they learn the shape of the crowd. Run a thousand simulated respondents and the marginal comes out plausible: a model can reproduce that sixty percent of people support something, landing near 0.60 at the group level, while assigning support and opposition to the wrong specific people. The population histogram looks right. The individuals underneath it are shuffled.

Getting the aggregate right does not mean getting anyone right.

Distributional competence and individual competence are different skills, and the category routinely reports the first while selling the second. That distinction is the whole reason a benchmark that scores individuals, not marginals, changes the picture.

Why they miss the individual

Run any of these architectures on the benchmark and individual accuracy lands around 0.36, below the 0.583 you get by always guessing the most common answer. This is not one vendor's bug. It is structural, and there are four reasons.

  • Distributional competence is not individual competence. A model can reproduce that sixty percent of people support something while getting the specific supporters and opponents wrong. Right aggregate, wrong people.
  • Demographic caricature. Personas flatten into stereotypes. The model answers as an "average 40-year-old Midwesterner" rather than the actual, idiosyncratic person, so within-group variation collapses and minorities get averaged away.
  • Collapsed variance and wording sensitivity. Answers cluster toward a safe modal response and shift with small rephrasings of the question, so real individual variability is both under-represented and unstable.
  • More context does not help. A leading small model scored lower with a full persona (0.299) than with demographics alone (0.328). If richer personal history makes the prediction worse, the model is not actually reasoning about the individual.

The tell is in the numbers everyone quotes. Independent benchmarks put individual accuracy on these tasks at roughly forty to fifty percent. Vendors advertise eighty-five to ninety-nine. The gap is not fraud, it is a metric swap: the group-level number, dressed as an individual one. A public miss on a high-profile election is what that gap looks like when a real decision rides on it.

What the machinery is for

Generative agents are a real advance for interactive, coherent simulation, and none of this says otherwise. The failure is not the architecture. It is selling distributional plausibility as individual prediction with no independent grade attached. That is why the ruler comes first. Once you can measure individual accuracy honestly, you can also use it as a training signal, which is the subject of how AlphaPrediction is trained.
