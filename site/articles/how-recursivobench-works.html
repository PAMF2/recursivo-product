How RecursivoBench works.

RecursivoBench is the independent ruler. It grades every human-behavior simulator against real people whose answers were hidden in advance, on public data anyone can rerun.

Every company selling synthetic respondents grades its own homework, and the grade is always an A, somewhere between 85 and 99 percent. None of those numbers come from an independent benchmark run on public ground truth. RecursivoBench is that benchmark. It takes real people, hides part of what they told us, asks a simulator to predict it, and scores the guess against the truth.

The ground truth

We build on Twin-2K-500, the largest public dataset of its kind, released by Columbia. It contains 2,058 real people, each answering roughly 500 questions across four survey waves that span behavioral economics, psychology, and politics. Waves one through three describe the person. Wave four is held out. Because the data is public and citable, anyone can reproduce a run without touching a vendor's private API.

From wave four we take 46 categorical items, each with a fixed set of answer options. Categorical items are what make grading unambiguous. There is exactly one real answer per person per item, so a prediction is either right or wrong. No free text, no rater judgment, no wiggle room.

The task

The task is deliberately simple, and it is the task every vendor implicitly claims to solve. Give a simulator everything a real person said in the first three waves, then ask it to predict how that same person answered one specific held-out wave-four question.

The simulator receives the persona, meaning the person's own prior answers, plus one held-out question and its options. It must return one of the options, as that person would. We then compare its choice to what the person actually chose. Nothing is inferred from a crowd. The unit of truth is one individual on one question.

How we score

We report two numbers for every simulator, because the category quietly reports one and lets buyers assume it is the other.

Individual accuracy is the fraction of predictions that exactly match the real person's answer, after light normalization. This is what a buyer actually needs: did the model get this person right? It is a per-person, per-question hit rate.

Group-level accuracy asks a much easier question: how closely the predicted distribution of answers matches the real distribution across people. We compute it as one minus the total-variation distance per item, averaged over items. It measures whether the crowd looks right in aggregate, not whether any single person was predicted correctly.

These are not the same measurement and they do not move together. A simulator can nail the overall split, say 60 percent support and 40 percent oppose, while assigning support and oppose to the wrong individuals. That is precisely what happens, and it is the gap the whole benchmark exists to expose.

By segment, held out by time

A single average hides where a model breaks. RecursivoBench stratifies every score by subgroup, so a buyer sees the gaps instead of a flattering mean. In our Claude baseline, individual accuracy varied by 8.7 points across age bands alone. A collapsed or caricatured minority can look fine in the aggregate and be badly wrong for the exact group you care about, which is why we report accuracy per age band and per gender, the maximum gap between groups, the worst-scoring items, and calibration.

Two guardrails keep the number honest. First, a temporal holdout: the persona comes from earlier waves and the graded answer comes from a later, unseen wave, so a simulator cannot have memorized the target. Second, no post-hoc filtering: we grade the full item set, not the questions where a model happened to shine. The ground truth is public, so anyone can rerun the exact same 46 items on the exact same 2,058 people and get the exact same number.

A score you cannot check is a claim, not a measurement. Grade against verifiable ground truth or you are just quoting yourself.

The result

Here is what the benchmark returns. The headline is not the one the category advertises. The best simulator we have tested is Claude Opus 4.8 with the full persona, at 0.539 individual accuracy. A model that ignores the persona entirely and always guesses the most common answer scores 0.583. The flagship loses to a constant.

The full ranking on individual accuracy makes the point sharper. Every simulator sits at or below the dumb baseline:

  • Baseline, majority class - 0.583. No persona, no model, just the most common answer every time.
  • Opus 4.8, full persona - 0.539. The best real simulator, and still short of the constant.
  • GPT-4.1, full persona - 0.360. More than twenty points under a guess that reads nothing.
  • GPT-4.1-mini, demographics only - 0.328, which beats the same model with the full persona at 0.299. More context about the person made the prediction worse.

Now look at the second column. On group-level accuracy, GPT-4.1 reaches 0.607, with GPT-4.1-mini on demographics at 0.570, Opus 4.8 at 0.556, and the full-persona mini at 0.501. That top figure lands right in the "about 60 percent" range vendors gesture at. It is real, and it is not the number a buyer's decision depends on. Individual is not distributional. Selling the second while the buyer hears the first is exactly the trick RecursivoBench was built to catch.

Independent work agrees on the ceiling. On SimBench, a separate effort that reports the same aggregate metric on other datasets, Claude 3.7 Sonnet scores 0.408 group-level, DeepSeek-R1 0.345, and the o4-mini reasoning variants 0.230 and 0.225, per arXiv 2510.17516. Different data, same story: nobody is close to predicting the individual.

That is the whole method. Real people, a held-out question, an exact-match grade reported two ways and broken down by segment, on public data anyone can check. Next, read how the category's simulators are built and why they miss the individual.
